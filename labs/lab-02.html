People Vote, Not the Land: The 2016 US Presidential Election, Mapped Four Ways

The question

Where was support for each party concentrated in the 2016 U.S. presidential election, and does that concentration look different depending on whether you measure it by counties, by votes, or by land?

The data

The layer is a county-level 2016 U.S. presidential election results feature layer **election_2016**, carrying DEM_votes, GOP_votes, DEM_per, GOP_per, and votes_total fields. There were three defects I could find inside the file itself:

  • An empty DEM_votes field. A subset of counties carries no Democratic vote count at all.
  • An alias that overclaims precision. DEM_per is stored as a 0–1 proportion (the national county average is 0.32 with a standard deviation of 0.15) but its field alias reads as a percentage.
  • Alaska duplicated 28×. The same Alaska row is duplicated 27 times beyond the one real record.

I later fixed the Alaska error by removing the 27 extra counts, which takes the raw sums (65,030,312 Democratic, 64,716,166 Republican) down to about 62,519,231 and 61,195,015. The winner doesn't change; the margin roughly quadruples, from ~314,000 to ~1,324,000 votes. What it can't fix is that the certified 2016 result was 65,853,514 Democratic to 62,984,828 Republican, so the corrected sums are still short.

The method

I built the map in ArcGIS Online Map Viewer, using the default web Mercator basemap. I first changed the layer to predominant category (using hue to indicate the winner of each county between GOP vs. DEM, darker for a larger margin). I then changed the layer's style to graduated symbol (using the size of the symbol to represent votes_total). I shifted to the standardized score method, where I used the following expression to calculate the z-score of each county against the national county average. 

// Lab 2 - standardised score for % DEM, 2016
//
//   z = (value - mean) / standard deviation
//
// The two constants come from the Statistics panel
// for DEM_per, with no filter applied.
 
var mean = 0.3174;
var sd   = 0.1527;
 
return ($feature.DEM_per - mean) / sd;

For the classification sweep, I styled the layer as Counts and Amounts (color) on DEM_per, turned on Classify data, and set 5 classes. I ran the same layer three times, changing only the method (Equal interval, Quantile, Natural breaks), holding extent and color ramp constant, and screenshotted each run. Finally, I used go-cart.io to create a state cartogram (using area resized by total votes cast, colored by Pct_DEM quintile to represent the statewide total votes and how much the states lean Democratic).

Four views of the same election

Reading the channels

Map Visual channel What it makes easy to see What it hides or distorts
1 · Predominant category Hue A clean binary read, county by county: which party's share was higher, and by how much (via shade). Says nothing about votes or population. A huge, sparse red county carries the same visual weight as a small, dense one — the classic "land doesn't vote" distortion.
2 · Graduated symbol Size Where the votes actually were. Large circles over Los Angeles, Chicago, and New York correct the choropleth's land-area bias. Says nothing about which party won. Circles in dense metro clusters overlap and merge, making individual counties hard to distinguish.
3 · Standardized score Value (lightness) How far a county's Democratic share sits from the comparison population's average, in standardized units rather than raw percentage points. Depends entirely on the reference population — here, all U.S. counties. Change it and every county's color changes with no change to the underlying votes. It also inherits the layer's own data-quality problems, since the average and std. dev. were computed over the flawed rows.
4 · Cartogram Area Corrects land-area distortion at the state level: high-population states like California and New York get appropriate visual weight, low-turnout states shrink. Recognizable state shapes are lost, so it's harder to orient. Aggregating to state quintiles also erases the county-level texture in maps 1–3 — a state can look uniform while containing a real mix of red rural and blue urban counties.

What I found

By predominant category, the GOP's share was higher in 2,555 counties, the DEM's in 408 — a 6-to-1 split that makes Figure 1 look overwhelmingly red. (The two counts add to 2,963, short of the ~3,143 U.S. counties — consistent with the empty DEM_votes field leaving some counties unclassifiable.) But a county count isn't a vote count: Figure 2's graduated symbols show a large share of actual votes sitting inside a small number of high-population counties — Los Angeles, Chicago, New York — that barely register as shapes in a county tally. Count counties and Republicans dominate; count votes and the picture is far closer.

Across the three schemes, the top class swung from 11 counties (natural breaks) to 43 (equal interval) to 626 (quantile) — a 50-fold range on identical data. Equal interval and natural breaks broadly agree (a small, distinct cluster of strongly Democratic counties); quantile disagrees not because the pattern changed, but because it always fills its top class with roughly a fifth of counties, wherever the values fall.

The scheme I'd publish

The scheme, named Equal interval. Its breaks are self-explanatory — "top class starts at 74.9%" is checkable without knowing how many counties qualify. Quantile always allocates the same fifth to the top class regardless of the data, so its map describes its own construction more than the vote pattern.

One alternative scheme, mapped, and what changed Quantile. Top class jumped from 43 counties to 626 (14.5×), threshold dropped from 74.9% to 43.3%. The map fills in far more blue, though most of those counties are only moderately Democratic, not the strongly Democratic counties equal interval isolates.
The claim you would still make if the scheme changed Democratic vote share is concentrated in a minority of counties, largely the same ones across schemes — natural breaks' 11 sit inside equal interval's 43. Only how large that minority looks changes: under 1% (natural breaks) up to a fifth (quantile) — never a majority, under any scheme.

Written reflection

I published equal interval and set aside natural breaks, which tells nearly the same story more dramatically: 11 counties above 82.7% Democratic share, versus 43 above 74.9%. Natural breaks isn't wrong — it finds a real gap, not an arbitrary width — but a reader who saw only that map, without the scheme named, couldn't tell whether "the top class" meant 11 counties, 43, or quantile's automatic 626. Equal interval's breaks are at least legible on their own.

The z-score map centers on 32%, the plain average of each county's Democratic share. That's not the national vote share (about 51%, once Alaska and the undercount are worked out against certified totals) — it's the average county, and most counties are small, rural, and lean Republican. Centering there makes a deeply red county the map's neutral baseline, so a county at 40% Democratic reads as blue-leaning even though the GOP carried it easily. I'd center the ramp on the vote-weighted national share instead, so the color break tracks who actually won the votes, not what the typical county looks like.

That choice has real stakes: campaigns use maps like this to decide where to open field offices. A planner reading a county at "40%, colored blue" could mistake it for persuadable, when it's really solid Republican — and the Democratic minority actually worth turning out there get read as already-won either way.

What this does not show

None of these maps show third-party voters or non-voters — DEM_per and GOP_per are shares of the two-party vote, not of eligible adults, so a closely-divided county can still have a large share of residents represented by neither color. The layer's own defects limit everything built from it: the empty DEM_votes field silently drops an unknown number of counties rather than counting them as zero, and the Alaska duplication — fixable from inside the file — sits alongside a deeper undercount (several million votes short of the certified result, by different amounts on each side) that this lab can't diagnose. Every vote total above should be read as approximate for that reason. The cartogram's state-level aggregation also means its color has none of the county-level resolution the other three maps do.
