Inside the Margins: Food Insecurity Among Migrants Without a Hukou, Ring by Ring from Huangpu

The question

Among migrants without a Shanghai hukou, was the share in food insecurity categories 3–4 different across 10 km bands outward from the Huangpu centroid?

Food insecurity categories 3 and 4 indicate moderate and severe food insecurity. I divided the city into four distance bands: 0–10, 10–20, 20–30, and 30+ km.

The data

The analysis uses the 2022 Shanghai Lockdown Study, an online survey conducted by NYU Shanghai from April 29 to June 1, 2022. The survey included 3,348 respondents, with about 200 respondents per district across 16 districts of Shanghai.

My analysis focuses on adult migrants without a Shanghai hukou who were in Shanghai during the final weeks of the 2022 lockdown and whose addresses could be geocoded. The findings do not represent all Shanghai residents or all migrants.

I used migration-status codes 2 and 3 for permanent and temporary migrants without a Shanghai hukou. Food insecurity was coded from 1 (food secure) to 4 (severely food insecure). The codebook does not explain code 0, so I excluded the eight respondents with that value, two of whom were otherwise in my analysis group.

The study's published figures are weighted, but my file did not include survey weights. My results are therefore unweighted shares within this sample. The study reported that 69% of all respondents were in categories 3–4. My figure of 77.6% applies to a different group—migrants without a hukou who had coordinates—and should not be compared directly with the published figure.

The method

I measured distances in QGIS and did the counting and the mapping in ArcGIS Online. Both layers were in EPSG:32651 (WGS 84 / UTM 51N) for the distance step, so every distance is straight-line and in metres.

My inclusion criteria were to include respondents with a coordinate, migration status 2 or 3, and Food insecurity different from 0. Then, I measure each point’s distance to the centre, sort the points into four bands, and recode food insecurity to 0 or 1. After counting the number of valid responses and the share of food-insecure respondents in each band, I compare the four shares beside their rough margins.

I set the center of the rings as the centroid of the Huangpu polygon, from Centroids in QGIS: (355554.4, 3454525.2) in EPSG:32651 since this centroid was closest to the geometric center of Shanghai compared to other districts.

I created a new field dist_km in the Field Calculator: distance($geometry, make_point(355554.4, 3454525.2)) / 1000

For the bands and the outcome. 0 < dist_km ≤ 10 is band 1, 10 < dist_km ≤ 20 band 2, 20 < dist_km ≤ 30 band 3, and anything beyond 30 km band 4. Food insecurity 1 and 2 became 0, and 3 and 4 became 1.

I uploaded the layer with dist_km to ArcGIS Online as a private working layer. For each band, I filtered it and read n from the record count and the positives from the Sum of values of the 0/1 field. The rough margin is 2 × √(p × (1 − p) / n).

For sensitivity checks, I did two reruns. First, I changed the width of the bands: from 10 km to 15 km, giving 0–15, 15–30, 30–45 and 45+ km. Then, I changed the categorical cut-off for food insecurity from categories 3–4 to categories 2–4.

To make the map in ArcGIS Map Viewer, I run Find Centroids on the districts and kept Huangpu, and Dissolve Boundaries on the 16 districts to get one municipal polygon. I then create Buffers on the centre at 10, 20, and 30 km to obtain three non-overlapping rings, and then run Overlay Layers → Intersect to clip them to the municipality. I run another Create Buffers at a single 30 km, then use Overlay Layers → Erase, with the municipality as input and the disc as the erase layer, leaving the 30+ band. I use Merge Layers to join the three rings and the outer band, with a text field band and a field band_order (1 to 4), and I added each band’s share by hand to draw the choropleth. For the district version, I run Summarize Within on the 1,610 respondents with the district polygons and classify the shares into five classes using a natural breaks algorithm.

The map

What I found

Unweighted shares from the 2022 Shanghai Lockdown Survey, a purposive online sample: 2,795 of 3,348 respondents had a coordinate, and 1,610 of those were migrants without a Shanghai hukou with a defined food insecurity code. Bands were defined by straight-line distance in EPSG:32651 from the centroid of Huangpu, measured in QGIS; the counts and the maps were made in ArcGIS Online. The share in categories 3–4 was 76.2, 78.3, 81.0 and 75.3 per cent from the inner band outward. In the main run every band’s rough margin overlaps all the others, so I report a possible peak at 20–30 km, not a gradient with distance from the centre.

Among the 1,610 migrants without a hukou, 77.6% (1,250) were in food insecurity categories 3–4. Across the four bands, the share ran from 75.3% in the outermost to 81.0% in the third, a spread of 5.7 points against margins of about ±4 points in each band. It rose over the first three bands and dropped in the fourth.

Districts instead of rings

The district map shows a different pattern from the distance-band map. Shares range from 68.7% to 82.0%, and the darkest district is in western Shanghai rather than forming a clear ring around Huangpu. With 1,610 respondents across 16 districts, the average would be about 100 respondents per district if they were evenly distributed. At that sample size, the rough margin is around ±8 percentage points—larger than the differences between many of the mapped classes. The map is therefore useful for checking whether the ring pattern misses a broader geographic pattern, but it should not be treated as a reliable ranking of districts.

What this does not show

The main result is a possible peak in food insecurity 20–30 km from the Huangpu centroid, not a clear distance gradient. The share in categories 3–4 was 81.0% in this band, compared with 76.2% in the inner band and 75.3% in the outer band. The rough ranges overlap, so the differences may reflect sampling variation.

The sensitivity checks showed a similar middle-band peak when I moved the boundary to 15 km and when I counted mild food insecurity as well. The wider food-insecurity definition produced a larger gap between the middle and inner bands, but the rough margins still rely on a random-sample assumption. The 45+ km band also had a lower share, but its 79 respondents are too few to support a strong conclusion.

One possible explanation for this pattern is the difference in food access across the city. The outer areas may have more agricultural activities and local food production, making some food more readily available. People living closer to the centre, on the other hand, may have greater purchasing power and better access to shops, services, and other resources. Migrants in the middle bands may fall between these two situations: they may have less access to locally produced food than those in outer areas, while also having fewer financial resources than people closer to the centre. This could help explain why food insecurity was highest in the middle bands. However, this is only a possible explanation, as my analysis did not directly measure agricultural activity, purchasing power, or access to food resources.

Several limitations affect how these findings should be read. The sample was recruited online and purposively, not randomly, so it does not represent all migrants in Shanghai. The 553 respondents without coordinates were excluded, and their food insecurity levels are unknown. Shared geocoder fallback coordinates may also have placed some respondents in the wrong band. In addition, the analysis measures straight-line distance from the centroid of Huangpu, not travel distance or distance from a commonly recognised city centre.

The choices used to create the bands also matter. The 10 km and 30 km boundaries were decisions made for this analysis, and changing them altered the pattern at the city's edge. The map buffers were created in ArcGIS Online, while respondent distances were calculated in QGIS; the centre point was calculated separately in the two programs, and I did not check whether any small difference changed a respondent's band.

Overall, the results suggest a middle-distance peak that could be tested with a stronger sample and a more formal statistical analysis. They do not show that distance from central Shanghai causes food insecurity, nor do they establish that the observed differences apply to migrants beyond this survey sample.
