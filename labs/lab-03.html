One Fire, Three Frames: How a Web Map Made the Soberanes Fire 54% Bigger

The question

How many square kilometres burned in the 2016 Soberanes Fire in Monterey County, California, and how much does that figure change with the projection it is measured in, the resolution of the imagery behind the boundary, and the terrain it burned across?

The data

  • Perimeter. Soberanes_Perimeter, an ArcGIS Online feature layer published by Teach_with_GIS (Esri) (item search). One feature, three fields: Id, Shape__Area, Shape__Length.
  • Imagery. Landsat archive in Esri's Landsat Explorer, short-wave infrared renderer, native cell size 30 m. Pre-fire scene 20 June 2016, post-fire scene 26 October 2016. The fire burned 22 July to 12 October 2016, so both dates fall outside it. The scenes are 128 days apart, exactly eight 16-day Landsat cycles, which is consistent with the same orbit track.
  • Elevation. The default world elevation surface in ArcGIS Scene Viewer, used for the 3D scene and the elevation profiles.
  • Reference figure. The incident record's 132,127 acres.

Two things I found inside the file:

  • The coordinate system is only in the service definition. Shape__Area (824,638,034 m²) and Shape__Length (556,808 m) look like facts about the fire. The service's spatialReference block reads wkid 102100 (latest WKID 3857), which epsg.io lists as WGS 84 / Pseudo-Mercator, i.e. Web Mercator. So both numbers are measurements on a Mercator sheet. The frame is not on the map, legend, pop-up or attribute table. I would have met it only when a figure failed a sanity check, if ever.
  • The polygon is a raster traced into vectors. The lab's own service query reports 690 rings in WGS 84 (755 in Web Mercator). The largest ring holds 99.986% of the area, and 686 rings are smaller than one 30 m Landsat cell (900 m²). These counts are the brief's, not mine; I did not rerun Count(Geometry($feature).rings).

The method

I opened the layer in ArcGIS Online Map Viewer and read the service definition at the layer's /query?where=1=1&outFields=*&returnGeometry=false&f=pjson endpoint. I then added this Arcade expression to the pop-up. Area does planar arithmetic on the stored (Web Mercator) coordinates; AreaGeodetic measures on the WGS 84 ellipsoid. 

// Two ways to measure one polygon.
var planar = Area($feature, 'square-kilometers');
var globe  = AreaGeodetic($feature, 'square-kilometers');
var acres  = AreaGeodetic($feature, 'acres');

return 'planar   ' + Round(planar, 3) + ' km2'
   + TextFormatting.NewLine
   + 'geodetic ' + Round(globe,  3) + ' km2'
   + TextFormatting.NewLine
   + 'geodetic ' + Round(acres,  0) + ' acres'
   + TextFormatting.NewLine
   + 'ratio    ' + Round(planar / globe, 4);

The pop-up returned planar 824.323 km², geodetic 533.891 km², 131,927 acres and ratio 1.544. I traced the burn by hand with Map Viewer's Measurement tool (twelve clicks; this tool measures geodetically, so it compares with AreaGeodetic). Planar UTM 10N (533.929 km²) and California Albers (534.182 km²) are the brief's given values, because a layer I don't own cannot be reprojected in ArcGIS Online.

Projection prediction. Web Mercator inflates linear scale by sec(φ) and area by sec²(φ). I computed sec²(φ) − 1 for six latitudes before comparing with the measurement. Grid band. I took the uncertainty in area as the geodetic perimeter (441.955 km) times the cell size, against the geodetic area (534.182 km², the brief's value, which is 0.05% above my own reading). Terrain. In Scene Viewer I drew two Elevation Profile lines across the burn, read the average slope of each, and multiplied the geodetic area by 1/cos(slope). Vertical datum. I read geoid separation N from the GeographicLib geoid calculator under EGM2008, EGM96 and EGM84, and converted heights with h = H + N. The conversion in the write-up uses EGM2008, the most recent of the three.

Four rows agree within 0.15%: geodetic, UTM, Albers and the agency figure. My hand trace is 1.6% off using twelve vertices and no care, while the Web Mercator row is 54% off. The error in this workflow lives in the frame, not in the tracing. The agency's figure agrees with the geodetic and equal-area rows, so it was almost certainly not computed in Web Mercator. I would have to read the incident record's metadata for the stated projection to be sure.

Prediction check. sec²(36.326°) = 1.5406. My measured ratio is 1.5440, within 0.2%, from one cosine. That licenses a rule for any web map: a planar area read at latitude φ is the true area times sec²(φ), a known multiple and not an unknown error, so it can be corrected by cos²(φ). The rule holds for small extents; the fire spans about a third of a degree of latitude, which is probably the 0.2%. The perimeters agree less cleanly (556.808 / 441.955 = 1.26; 1.26² = 1.59, against 1.54 for areas), for the same reason.

What else has this affected? In Lab 2, I read the predominant-party map by how much red or blue covered the page. At Montana's latitude (about 47°) Web Mercator inflates area by about 115% (sec² = 2.15), so northern counties looked more than twice as large as southern ones of equal size.

At Landsat resolution the grid band is 2.5% of the area and the projection error is 54%, so the frame is more than twenty times the grid. At 500 m the band (41%) approaches the projection error. The ranking is a fact about this fire, this latitude and this sensor. It would flip near the equator, where the projection term is zero and even a Landsat band would outrank it.

The boundary is a staircase. Every decision about where burned ground stops was made one cell at a time. A feature narrower than a cell either vanishes or fills a whole cell, and the file does not say which. I did not measure a finger of burn against the 30 m cell in Map Viewer, so I make no claim about a specific finger. The structure of the file shows the same thing. The isoperimetric ratio 4πA/P² is 0.0334 for the stored Web Mercator pair and 0.0344 for the geodetic pair, where a circle gives 1. A circle of 534 km² would have a circumference of about 82 km, and the file claims 442 km. The stored geometry records detail the sensor could not have seen. Area is robust to this and perimeter is not, so I would publish the area and publish the perimeter only with its resolution stated beside it.

With h = H + N, a receiver reporting an ellipsoidal height of 900 m at the fire sits at H = 900 − (−32.978) = 932.98 m orthometric under EGM2008. EGM96 gives 934.08 m and EGM84 934.76 m. The conversion needs a model, and the choice of model moves the answer by up to 1.8 m, so the model belongs in the methods. Runoff follows gravity, not an ellipsoid, so a drainage model needs the orthometric height H. Using the ellipsoidal height would put the surface tens of metres off, 33 m at the fire, and the error varies across the area.

Terrain: area on a slope

My two elevation profiles gave average slopes of 20.7° and 19.7°, so the correction factors are 1.0690 and 1.0622. Applied to the geodetic area of 534.182 km², the ground surface is at least 567.4 to 571.0 km², about 6 to 7% more than the planimetric shadow. This is a lower bound, because applying the cosine to an average slope understates surface area on steep ground. Of the three effects the terrain is second: 6 to 7% against 54% for projection and 2.5% for the grid.

One mountain, two numbers

Nepal's pre-2020 height was 8,848 m (Survey of India, 1954), measured to the snow and ice on top. China's 2005 survey gave 8,844.43 m, measured to the rock beneath. The two governments announced a joint figure of 8,848.86 m on 8 December 2020. The gap is 3.57 m, and China's own 2005 survey put the snow and ice at about 3.5 m, so almost all of it was disagreement about what "height" means, not about measurement quality.

Written reflection

I would publish 533.9 km² (about 131,900 acres), measured geodetically on WGS 84 from a 30 m boundary. Before publishing I would confirm the layer's spatialReference and check the result against an equal-area projection and the agency's 132,127 acres; four independent rows agree within 0.15%. If I had trusted the attribute table I would have published 824.6 km², 54% too high.

The ranking here is projection (54%), then terrain (6 to 7%), then grid (2.5%). It would flip near the equator, where projection error vanishes, on steeper ground, or with a 500 m product, whose band is 41%. Terrain is the one I could not have found from inside the file. The projection is in the service definition and the grid leaves traces, such as 686 rings smaller than a cell. A two-dimensional polygon carries no elevation at all.

Suppression cost is the use I would worry about. Soberanes cost about $260 million. Divided by the display-frame acreage it comes to about $1,280 per acre; divided by the real acreage, about $1,970. Fire-agency budget planners who benchmark against that figure would plan for suppression costs 35% too low, and the next large fire would be under-funded.

What this does not show

Resolution. The perimeter cannot be located more precisely than one 30 m cell, so anything it implies below that scale is an artefact. The perimeter length (442 km) is not a measurement I would stand behind. Terrain. The slope correction is a lower bound from two profile lines, not a surface integral over the whole burn. Vertical datum. The geoid models differ by up to 1.96 m, and I used EGM2008 without validating it against ground control. The 686-ring and 690-ring counts come from the lab brief and were not rerun. I did not trace or measure a narrow finger against the cell size, and I did not explain the 0.04% gap between Shape__Area and Area(). Finally, the Landsat scenes are four months apart, so the scar could include post-fire change, and I did not classify it; the perimeter is taken as published.
